# Issue Dossier

## 1. What Happened
In M/s Circar Paper Mills Ltd. v. District Collector, Nellore District, 2026 INSC 924, the Supreme Court held that the State could not use summary proceedings to evict occupants where ownership was genuinely disputed. The State claimed land associated with a paper mill was government-assigned land; the competing private claim relied on a decades-long chain of registered transactions, revenue entries, industrial possession, liquidation proceedings, and a court-supervised auction.

The Court restored the Company Judge's auction order and the purchaser's possession, while reviving connected writ proceedings so rival claims could be adjudicated in the proper forum. It applied Government of Andhra Pradesh v. Thummala Krishna Rao, which says summary eviction is unsuitable for bona fide title disputes. The judgment was delivered on 25 August and published on 29 August 2026.

## 2. Background
The controversy concerned about 40.65 acres within a larger tract in Nellore district. The private claim traced transactions and possession to the early twentieth century. Circar Paper Mills acquired land in 1980, established a factory, and later entered liquidation. The Official Liquidator took possession in 2001, and the Company Court approved an auction in favour of J.K. Paper Mills for ₹7.8 crore. The State invoked the Andhra Pradesh Assigned Lands (Prohibition of Transfers) Act, 1977.

The Act protects assigned land and authorises restoration in appropriate cases. But summary process cannot substitute for civil adjudication where the government's own title is seriously contested on credible material. The key distinction is between removing an unauthorised occupant from clearly established public land and deciding a complex ownership question.

## 5. Legal & Constitutional Significance
- **Rule of law** requires the executive to establish contested title through adjudication rather than make itself judge of a serious ownership dispute.
- Summary eviction statutes address clear unauthorised occupation; they do not confer a general power to decide complex title.
- The Court treated the 1977 Act's restoration mechanism as comparable to the summary power considered in Thummala Krishna Rao.
- Article 300A protects against deprivation of property except by authority of law. Fair use of lawful procedure is central to property deprivation.
- The Court did **not** finally declare private ownership merely because possession was old. It rejected the summary route and restored the legal proceedings.

## 6. Static GK Connection
- Article 300A is a constitutional, not fundamental, right; the Forty-Fourth Amendment removed the former fundamental right to property.
- Land is primarily in the State List, while acquisition and requisitioning of property is in the Concurrent List.
- A **bona fide dispute** is a genuine dispute supported by material, not an invented objection.
- **Summary proceedings** use an expedited procedure and are unsuitable where extensive title evidence is required.
- A liquidator collects and realises a company's assets under court supervision for lawful distribution.

## 7. CLAT Passage
Government may use a statutory summary process when public ownership is clear and an occupant lacks authority. A different problem arises where registered deeds, revenue records, long industrial possession, liquidation proceedings, and a judicial auction support a competing private claim. In Circar Paper Mills, the Supreme Court held that the executive could not decide such a bona fide title dispute through abbreviated assigned-lands machinery. The proper forum must adjudicate rival titles. The ruling did not hold that long possession creates ownership or finally settle title; it restored the Company Court's auction order and revived connected proceedings.

## 12. Confusion Traps
- **Possession vs title**: Long possession can make a claim substantial but does not itself prove ownership.
- **Procedure vs declaration**: The Court restricted summary procedure; it did not conclusively decide every title issue.
- **Constitutional vs fundamental right**: Property is protected by Article 300A but is not in Part III.
- **Invalid action vs invalid statute**: Use of the Act here was rejected; the Act itself was not struck down.
