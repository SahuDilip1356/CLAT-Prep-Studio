# Issue Dossier

## 1. What Happened
On 21 August 2026, the Supreme Court in **Rehana Khan v. Rizwan Siddiquee, 2026 INSC 907** upheld the Bar Council of India's order suspending an advocate from practice for two years. The Court held that professional confidence does not disappear merely because the lawyer-client relationship ends or the former client and advocate become adversaries.

The advocate had disclosed alleged client communications through media interactions after a dispute. The Court distinguished lawful self-defence from public disclosure: a lawyer who is falsely accused may approach investigating authorities, produce material in a proper legal proceeding and pursue remedies such as defamation, but cannot use television or other public platforms to reveal privileged or confidential communications. It dismissed cross-appeals by both sides and, because of their conduct in the litigation, directed each side to pay ₹5 lakh to the Supreme Court Legal Services Committee.

## 2. Background
Advocacy is a regulated profession built on candour between client and counsel. A client must be able to give the lawyer complete instructions without fearing later publicity. This confidence is protected by professional-conduct rules and by the law of evidence.

The Indian Evidence Act, 1872 protected professional communications in Section 126. The corresponding rule is now found in **Section 132 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023**, which took effect on 1 July 2024. It bars an advocate, unless the client expressly consents, from disclosing specified professional communications or advice. The protection continues after the professional engagement ends, but it does not cover communications made in furtherance of an illegal purpose or facts observed by the advocate showing that a crime or fraud occurred after the engagement began.

## 5. Legal & Constitutional Significance
- **Section 35, Advocates Act, 1961** authorises a State Bar Council's disciplinary committee, after due process, to dismiss a complaint, reprimand the advocate, suspend practice or remove the advocate's name from the State roll.
- **Sections 37 and 38, Advocates Act** provide appellate routes from a State Bar Council to the BCI and from the BCI to the Supreme Court.
- **Section 132, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023** protects communications made in the course and for the purpose of professional service, advice given and document contents or condition learned professionally. The duty continues after the engagement ends.
- The statutory exceptions are precise: no privilege attaches to a communication made in furtherance of an illegal purpose or to a fact observed by the advocate showing a crime or fraud committed after the engagement began.
- **Part VI, Chapter II of the BCI Rules** contains professional standards. The duty to the client coexists with an advocate's duty to court and law; it is not a licence to assist illegality.
- **Article 19(1)(g)** protects the right to practise a profession, but it is subject to reasonable restrictions and the Advocates Act's disciplinary regime. A suspension must therefore rest on lawful procedure and proved misconduct.

## 6. Static GK Connection
- The Advocates Act established one unified class of legal practitioners called **advocates**.
- The BCI is constituted under **Section 4** of the Advocates Act; State Bar Councils are constituted under Section 3.
- Legal professional privilege belongs primarily to the **client**, not to the advocate, so the advocate cannot waive it unilaterally.
- Confidentiality is broader as a professional duty; evidentiary privilege determines whether protected material may be compelled or disclosed in legal proceedings.
- The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam is one of the three new criminal-law statutes that came into force on 1 July 2024, alongside the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita and Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita.

## 7. CLAT Passage
An advocate and a former client became adversaries after their professional relationship ended. The advocate alleged that the former client had falsely implicated her and discussed material from their earlier communications in televised interviews. The Supreme Court accepted that an advocate is not defenceless: she may report an offence to competent investigators, rely on relevant material in a lawful proceeding and sue for defamation. It nevertheless upheld professional discipline because broadcasting client communications was not necessary to obtain those remedies. The Court explained that the statutory duty continues after employment ceases. That continuity encourages clients to speak candidly and preserves confidence in the justice system. The rule is not absolute, because the evidence statute excludes communications made to further an illegal purpose and later-observed facts showing crime or fraud.

## 10. One-Pager Revision
- **30-second summary**: Rehana Khan v. Rizwan Siddiquee upheld a two-year professional suspension and ruled that an advocate cannot publicise former-client confidences merely because the relationship has turned hostile. Lawful complaints and court remedies remain available.
- **Statute map**: Advocates Act Sections 35, 37 and 38 = discipline and appeals; BSA Section 132 = professional communications.
- **Continuity rule**: The confidentiality obligation survives termination of employment.
- **Exception rule**: Illegal-purpose communications and facts showing post-engagement crime or fraud are not protected.
- **Exam trap**: Confidentiality does not immunise crime or fraud, and it does not prevent an advocate from using lawful remedies.
- **Media trap**: A public interview is not equivalent to disclosure before a competent investigator or court.
- **Mnemonic**: **132 stays after you** — Section 132 survives the end of the lawyer-client relationship.

## 12. Confusion Traps
- **Confidentiality vs privilege**: Confidentiality is a professional obligation; privilege is also an evidentiary protection controlled by the client.
- **Termination vs waiver**: Ending the engagement does not waive protection. Express client consent is different.
- **Defence vs publicity**: A lawful complaint or pleading may be justified; broadcasting confidential material is not automatically justified.
- **Past wrongdoing vs future illegal purpose**: Seeking advice about past conduct is not the same as asking a lawyer to further illegality.
- **BCI vs State Bar Council**: State councils ordinarily start discipline; the BCI has appellate and other statutory functions, with a further appeal to the Supreme Court.
