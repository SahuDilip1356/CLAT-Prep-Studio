# Issue Dossier

## 1. What Happened
Parliament completed passage of the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026 after the Rajya Sabha passed it on 5 August 2026. The Lok Sabha had passed it on 3 August. The Bill replaces the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Ordinance, 2026, promulgated on 16 May, and substitutes “thirty-seven” for “thirty-three” in section 2 of the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956. Those figures exclude the Chief Justice of India, so the Court's maximum sanctioned strength is 38 including the CJI. The Bill deems the amendment to have operated from 16 May 2026 and preserves action already taken under the Ordinance. It does not create regional Supreme Court benches, change the collegium, or itself guarantee faster disposal of cases.

## 2. Background
Article 124(1) of the Constitution establishes the Supreme Court and permits Parliament by law to prescribe a larger number of judges than the original constitutional maximum. Parliament exercised that power through the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956 and has amended the statutory ceiling as the Court's workload grew. The 2019 amendment raised the number of judges other than the CJI from 30 to 33, producing a total sanctioned strength of 34. In May 2026, while Parliament was not in session, the President promulgated an Ordinance raising that number to 37. Because an Ordinance is temporary legislation, the government introduced a Bill during the Monsoon Session to replace it. The Bill's Statement of Objects and Reasons recorded 92,101 pending Supreme Court cases on 1 January 2026, with 75,410 fresh matters instituted and 65,615 disposed of during 2025. It argued that four more posts would help address backlog and enable the CJI to constitute Constitution Benches more regularly.

## 3. Timeline
- **1950**: Article 124(1) brought the Supreme Court into existence with a CJI and not more than seven other judges unless Parliament prescribed a larger number by law.
- **1956**: Parliament enacted the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956 to fix the statutory maximum of judges other than the CJI.
- **9 August 2019**: The 2019 amendment received presidential assent and raised the number of judges other than the CJI from 30 to 33.
- **16 May 2026**: The President promulgated the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Ordinance, 2026, raising the number other than the CJI from 33 to 37.
- **20 July 2026**: The replacement Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha as Bill No. 128 of 2026.
- **3 August 2026**: The Lok Sabha passed the Bill.
- **5 August 2026**: The Rajya Sabha passed the Bill, completing parliamentary passage.
- **After passage**: Presidential assent and publication as an Act remain distinct formal steps; the Bill's saving clause preserves actions taken under the Ordinance.

## 4. Key People & Organisations
- **Parliament of India**: Uses the power recognised by Article 124(1) to prescribe the maximum number of Supreme Court judges by ordinary legislation.
- **President of India**: Promulgated the May 2026 Ordinance under Article 123 and considers the Bill for assent under Article 111.
- **Chief Justice of India**: Heads the Supreme Court, constitutes benches, and had sought an increase in sanctioned strength because of rising filings and the demands of larger benches.
- **Ministry of Law and Justice**: Sponsored the Ordinance and Bill; Minister of State Arjun Ram Meghwal introduced and defended the measure.
- **Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha**: Passed the replacement Bill on 3 and 5 August 2026 respectively.
- **Supreme Court of India**: Its sanctioned strength, working strength, case-management capacity, and constitutional role are the subjects of the reform.

## 5. Legal & Constitutional Significance
The measure illustrates how the Constitution distributes responsibility for the structure and functioning of the higher judiciary.

- **Article 124(1)**: Creates the Supreme Court and allows Parliament by law to prescribe a larger number of judges; the numerical ceiling is statutory, not permanently frozen in the Constitution.
- **Article 123**: Permits the President to promulgate an Ordinance when both Houses are not in session and immediate action is considered necessary. An Ordinance has the force of an Act but is temporary and must face Parliament.
- **Article 111**: Governs presidential assent to Bills passed by Parliament; parliamentary passage and enactment are legally distinct stages.
- **Article 130**: Provides that the Supreme Court shall sit in Delhi or at another place appointed by the CJI with the President's approval. The 2026 Bill does not amend this rule or establish a regional bench.
- **Article 145(3)**: Requires at least five judges to decide a case involving a substantial question of law as to constitutional interpretation or a presidential reference under Article 143.
- **Article 21 and access to justice**: Delay can impair liberty and effective remedies, but increasing sanctioned strength is an administrative response rather than a declaration that every delayed case violates Article 21.
- **Ordinance replacement and savings**: The Bill repeals the May Ordinance while deeming the amendment effective from 16 May and preserving action already taken, preventing a legal gap.
- **Sanctioned versus working strength**: Raising the statutory ceiling creates posts; actual appointments still follow the constitutional appointments process and vacancies may remain.

## 6. Static GK Connection
The issue links constitutional design, parliamentary law-making, judicial administration, and court structure.

- **Supreme Court inauguration**: The Court began functioning on 28 January 1950 and sits in New Delhi.
- **Original strength**: Article 124 originally contemplated the CJI and up to seven other judges, for a total of eight.
- **2026 ceiling**: The amendment provides for 37 judges other than the CJI, or 38 including the CJI.
- **Constitution Bench**: Under Article 145(3), at least five judges hear substantial constitutional-interpretation questions and Article 143 references.
- **Judicial appointments**: Supreme Court judges are appointed by the President under Article 124; changing the numerical ceiling is not the same as changing the collegium system.
- **Retirement age**: A Supreme Court judge retires at 65, while a High Court judge retires at 62.
- **Ordinary Bill**: The judge-strength amendment changes an ordinary statute and does not require the special-majority procedure used for a constitutional amendment under Article 368.

## 7. CLAT Passage
Parliament has passed the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Bill, 2026 to replace an Ordinance promulgated in May. The Bill changes section 2 of the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956 by substituting 37 for 33 as the maximum number of judges other than the Chief Justice of India. The maximum sanctioned strength is therefore 38 when the CJI is included. Article 124(1) permits Parliament by law to prescribe this larger number; the Constitution itself need not be amended whenever the statutory ceiling changes. The Bill deems the change effective from 16 May 2026 and preserves steps already taken under the Ordinance. Its stated justification is the persistent gap between cases instituted and disposed of. On 1 January 2026, 92,101 cases were pending. During 2025, 75,410 fresh matters were instituted while 65,615 were disposed of. A larger court may also make it easier for the CJI to constitute benches of at least five judges for substantial constitutional questions under Article 145(3), without removing as many judges from regular benches. Yet sanctioned strength is only a ceiling. It does not itself fill vacancies, alter the appointments process, create regional benches, or guarantee that pendency will fall. Regional sittings remain governed by Article 130, under which the Court sits in Delhi or at another place appointed by the CJI with the President's approval.

### Questions
1. **Why did the 2026 measure not require a constitutional amendment?**:
   - (A) Article 124(1) authorises Parliament to prescribe a larger number of Supreme Court judges by law.
   - (B) The Chief Justice may amend any statute without Parliament.
   - (C) Article 368 never applies to changes concerning courts.
   - (D) An Ordinance permanently changes the Constitution.
   - *Correct Answer*: A
   - *Explanation*: The passage states that Article 124(1) leaves the numerical ceiling to parliamentary legislation.
2. **Which inference about pendency is best supported by the passage?**:
   - (A) Adding four posts automatically disposes of all old cases.
   - (B) The measure may add institutional capacity, but appointments and case management still affect actual disposal.
   - (C) Pendency is caused only by Constitution Benches.
   - (D) The Bill abolishes appeals to the Supreme Court.
   - *Correct Answer*: B
   - *Explanation*: The passage distinguishes a higher sanctioned ceiling from filled posts and actual case disposal.
3. **Which reform was not made by the Bill described in the passage?**:
   - (A) Raising the number of judges other than the CJI to 37.
   - (B) Preserving action taken under the May Ordinance.
   - (C) Establishing regional Supreme Court benches.
   - (D) Replacing the Ordinance through parliamentary legislation.
   - *Correct Answer*: C
   - *Explanation*: Regional sittings remain governed by Article 130; the 2026 Bill only amends the statutory judge ceiling.

## 12. Confusion Traps
- **Frequently confused with**: Increasing Supreme Court judge strength versus creating a National Court of Appeal or regional Supreme Court benches.
- **Why they differ**: The 2026 amendment changes only the maximum number of Supreme Court judges. A separate appellate tier or regional sitting would require distinct legal and institutional action.
- **Memory clue**: “More judges is not a new court.”
