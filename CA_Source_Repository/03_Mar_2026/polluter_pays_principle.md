# Issue Dossier

## 1. What Happened
On 22 August 2026, the Supreme Court allowed **Vyakti Vikas Kendra India's** appeal arising from the 2016 World Culture Festival on the Yamuna floodplain. It set aside the National Green Tribunal's common judgment of 7 December 2017 and consequential proceedings against the appellant, and directed the Delhi Development Authority to refund the **₹5 crore** deposited by the appellant within four weeks.

The Court did not weaken the polluter-pays principle. It held that the principle has enduring force and that Section 17 of the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010 is one of its statutory expressions. But before private liability is imposed, the authority must establish actual environmental damage and a causal link between that damage and the alleged polluter. On the evidence, the Court found that this link had not been proved and that the site was already in a degraded condition before the event.

## 2. Background
The Art of Living-associated appellant organised the World Culture Festival on the Yamuna floodplain in Delhi in March 2016 after receiving permission from the DDA. Proceedings before the NGT alleged damage to the riverine ecology. Interim directions led to a ₹5 crore deposit, and an expert committee assessed environmental impacts and proposed extensive remedial work.

The NGT's 2017 final decision relied substantially on interim findings and the expert material. The appellant challenged that outcome under Section 22 of the NGT Act. The Supreme Court examined not only whether ecological harm existed, but also who legally caused it, what evidence could support liability, and whether the NGT had kept restoration within its statutory jurisdiction.

## 5. Legal & Constitutional Significance
- **Polluter pays requires a polluter**: Environmental liability cannot rest on the label alone. The decision-maker must establish damage and causation connecting the respondent's conduct to that damage.
- **Section 17, NGT Act**: Provides relief and compensation where damage results from an accident or the adverse impact of an activity or operation. Section 17(3)'s no-fault rule applies where an accident is involved; the Court held that this case was not an accident case.
- **Section 15, NGT Act**: Authorises relief, compensation and restitution. The Court distinguished **restoration**, which seeks return to the earlier condition, from broader **rehabilitation**, which may aim to make an ecosystem fully functional or optimise ecological services.
- **Fair procedure and evidence**: Interim findings cannot simply become final without being tested against the complete record. Expert opinion must be objective, reasoned and open to rebuttal.
- **Public-trust doctrine**: Natural resources such as river floodplains are held by the State in trust for the public. A public authority cannot shift its wider custodial duty to a private party without legally proving that party's responsibility.
- **Precautionary principle and intergenerational equity**: DDA should have applied ecological caution before granting permission and must continue rehabilitation for present and future generations.
- **Articles 21, 48A and 51A(g)**: Environmental jurisprudence connects the right to life, the State's directive to protect the environment and the citizen's fundamental duty toward nature.

## 6. Static GK Connection
- The **National Green Tribunal Act, 2010** created a specialist tribunal for effective and expeditious disposal of environmental cases.
- Under **Section 20**, the NGT must apply sustainable development, the precautionary principle and polluter pays while deciding cases.
- An appeal from an NGT award, decision or order lies to the Supreme Court under **Section 22**, ordinarily within ninety days.
- The principal bench of the NGT is in **New Delhi**.
- A **floodplain** is land beside a river that is periodically inundated and performs functions such as carrying floodwater, supporting habitat and enabling groundwater recharge.
- Polluter pays concerns the cost of preventing and remedying pollution; it is not a licence to pollute after paying a fee.

## 7. CLAT Passage
An environmental tribunal directed an event organiser to finance extensive work on a degraded river floodplain. The organiser had obtained permission from the public land-owning authority. Expert material described ecological harm, but the final order largely carried forward findings made at an interim stage and did not establish which part of the harm was caused by the event rather than by the site's earlier condition. On appeal, the Supreme Court affirmed that polluter pays remains a central environmental principle. It nevertheless held that the alleged person must first be shown to be a polluter through proof of damage and a causal connection. The Court also distinguished restoration of a site from a wider rehabilitation programme and said a tribunal cannot transfer a public authority's broad custodial burden to a private party without statutory and evidentiary justification.

### Questions
1. **Which proposition best reflects the Supreme Court's approach to polluter pays?**:
   - (A) The principle was abolished whenever a public authority grants permission.
   - (B) Liability may be imposed without identifying damage or causation.
   - (C) The principle remains valid, but the respondent must first be proved to be a polluter.
   - (D) Environmental liability can arise only after a criminal conviction.
   - *Correct Answer*: C
   - *Explanation*: The Court preserved the principle while insisting on proof of damage and a causal link to the alleged polluter.
2. **Why was reliance on the interim findings legally problematic?**:
   - (A) Interim findings can never concern environmental disputes.
   - (B) They were treated as final without adequate testing against the complete evidence and objections.
   - (C) Only Parliament may read an expert report.
   - (D) The NGT Act prohibits all expert assistance.
   - *Correct Answer*: B
   - *Explanation*: Final liability requires a reasoned assessment of the full record, not automatic adoption of provisional conclusions.
3. **Which inference follows from the Court's treatment of DDA?**:
   - (A) A public authority's custodial duty disappears after it grants event permission.
   - (B) DDA may continue floodplain rehabilitation even though private liability was not proved.
   - (C) River floodplains are exclusively private property.
   - (D) Rehabilitation and a monetary penalty are always identical.
   - *Correct Answer*: B
   - *Explanation*: Setting aside liability against the appellant did not extinguish DDA's public-trust responsibility for the floodplain.

## 12. Confusion Traps
- **Polluter pays vs pay-to-pollute**: The doctrine allocates prevention and remediation costs; payment does not authorise pollution.
- **Ecological harm vs legal attribution**: Evidence that a site is damaged does not by itself prove which respondent caused the damage.
- **No-fault liability vs no causation**: No-fault rules can remove the need to prove negligence; they do not eliminate statutory scope and causal attribution.
- **Restoration vs rehabilitation**: The terms overlap in ordinary speech but can differ in the breadth of the remedy ordered.
- **Private liability vs public duty**: Failure to prove liability against the organiser does not release DDA from its custodial obligation.
- **Interim vs final finding**: A provisional conclusion must still be tested before it becomes the foundation of final liability.
