# Issue Dossier

## 1. What Happened
On 1 September 2026, the Supreme Court decided **M/s Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Union of India**, 2026 INSC 933. The airline had collected the now-discontinued Foreign Travel Tax (FTT) but deposited it late on six occasions. Authorities treated the delay as failure to pay under Section 38(3) of the Finance Act, 1979 and ultimately increased an original ₹12,000 penalty to ₹71,29,140 after remand.

The Court held that delayed deposit fell under **Section 38(4)** read with Rules 4 and 9 of the Foreign Travel Tax Rules, 1979, not Section 38(3). Penalty was discretionary rather than automatic, and the authorities had overlooked explanations that could support condonation. It set aside the penalty, ordered refund of any amount paid with **9% annual interest within three months**, and discharged the bank guarantee.

## 2. Background
Foreign Travel Tax was imposed under Chapter V of the Finance Act, 1979 on international journeys and collected through carriers. The statutory framework distinguished non-payment from delayed payment and empowered customs authorities to adjudicate penalties. Rule 4 allowed time-related relief in appropriate circumstances.

After the airline appealed an initial ₹12,000 penalty, the matter was remanded and the penalty rose to more than ₹71 lakh. This raised **no reformatio in peius**: using a legal remedy should not put an appellant in a worse position merely because it appealed. The Court linked the principle to fair procedure, natural justice and equity.

## 5. Legal & Constitutional Significance
- **Classification of breach**: “Failure to pay” and “delay in paying” are not interchangeable when legislation provides different consequences.
- **Penalty not automatic**: A breach triggers jurisdiction to consider penalty; it does not necessarily compel punishment without considering discretion and explanation.
- **Strict construction**: Fiscal penalty provisions are read according to their text, especially where different sub-sections specify different defaults.
- **Natural justice**: Reasons and relevant explanations must be considered; statutory discretion cannot be replaced by an automatic formula.
- **No reformatio in peius**: An appellant should not be placed in a worse position merely because it used an appellate remedy. The Court described this as a procedural guarantee and principle of equity and natural justice.
- **Article 14 context**: Non-arbitrariness informs the exercise of administrative penalty discretion, although the Court resolved the appeal through statutory interpretation rather than striking down Section 38(3).

## 6. Static GK Connection
- **Tax vs penalty**: Tax raises revenue; penalty is a consequence imposed for breach and depends on statutory conditions.
- **Interest vs penalty**: Interest commonly compensates for delayed use of money; penalty is punitive or deterrent.
- **Writ of certiorari**: A constitutional remedy used to quash unlawful orders of public authorities.
- **Natural justice**: Includes fair hearing, absence of bias and reasoned consideration.
- **Latin**: *Reformatio in peius* means a change for the worse; *no reformatio in peius* bars worsening an appellant's position merely because it appealed.

## 7. CLAT Passage
A statute separately addressed failure to pay a collected tax and delay in depositing it. An airline deposited the tax late on six occasions and explained the delays, but the authorities applied the harsher failure-to-pay provision. After the airline appealed an initial ₹12,000 penalty, a remand resulted in a penalty exceeding ₹71 lakh.

The Supreme Court held that the correct provision was the one dealing with delayed deposit. It also found that penalty was not automatic: the decision-maker had to exercise statutory discretion and consider whether the delay should be condoned. Finally, the Court invoked no reformatio in peius, under which a litigant should not be made worse off merely for using an appellate remedy. This principle supports fair procedure and natural justice by ensuring that access to appeal does not itself become a source of punishment.

### Questions
1. **Why was the harsher provision inapplicable?**:
   - (A) Airlines are constitutionally immune from tax.
   - (B) The facts concerned delayed deposit, not failure to pay.
   - (C) Customs officers cannot administer tax laws.
   - (D) The Finance Act had never been enacted.
   - *Correct Answer*: B
   - *Explanation*: The statutory scheme distinguished delay from failure to pay.
2. **What does no reformatio in peius protect against?**:
   - (A) Any judicial interpretation of Latin terms
   - (B) A litigant becoming worse off merely because it appealed
   - (C) Payment of compensatory interest
   - (D) Legislative amendment after an appeal
   - *Correct Answer*: B
   - *Explanation*: The principle protects fair use of appellate remedies.
3. **Why was penalty not automatic?**:
   - (A) The statute vested discretion in the adjudicating officer.
   - (B) Every tax penalty is unconstitutional.
   - (C) Only Parliament may decide individual cases.
   - (D) Delay always extinguishes tax liability.
   - *Correct Answer*: A
   - *Explanation*: The officer had to decide whether penalty was warranted after considering relevant circumstances.

## 10. One-Pager Revision
- **Case**: Saudi Arabian Airlines v Union of India, 2026 INSC 933.
- **Issue**: Six delayed FTT deposits.
- **Correct law**: Section 38(4), not failure-to-pay Section 38(3).
- **Rule**: Penalty is not automatic; discretion and explanations matter.
- **Appeal principle**: No reformatio in peius.
- **Numbers**: ₹12,000 became ₹71,29,140 after remand.
- **Relief**: Penalty quashed; refund with 9% interest within three months.

## 12. Confusion Traps
- **Failure vs delay**: Late remittance is not automatically complete non-payment.
- **Tax vs penalty**: Setting aside penalty does not imply that collected tax need not be deposited.
- **Discretion vs immunity**: Discretion requires lawful consideration; it does not guarantee no penalty in every delayed-payment case.
- **Remand vs licence to enhance**: A remand does not by itself justify punishing someone for appealing.
- **Statutory interpretation vs invalidation**: The Court did not need to strike down Section 38(3) to allow the appeal.
